# The Mac mini speeds everything but Dreamer

A Mac mini with the M6 chip and 32 GB of memory should run Jack's world and his simple replay brain (brain B, soft actor-critic) about **4.5 to 8 times faster than the whole 4-core cloud server, and 10 to 16 times faster than a free Kaggle CPU session**. At that speed brain B's whole decisive test (20 runs of one million decisions each) takes **about 4 to 40 hours of the Mac, most likely under a day**. The Dreamer-style brain (brain A) is a different story. Hour for hour the Mac's graphics chip is **slower than the free NVIDIA T4: about 0.2 to 0.6 times its speed with PyTorch, and perhaps 0.3 to 1 times with Apple's MLX library**, for which no Dreamer code exists yet. The Mac makes up the gap by running 168 hours a week where the T4 gives 30. Run on its own, a 0.64-million-parameter Dreamer run at the published training rate takes **about 1 to 4 days**, and twenty of them take **3 to 11 weeks**. With the T4 running alongside, the twenty take **about 2 to 5 weeks**. The 9.7-million-parameter Dreamer at the full rate is out of reach of every free or owned machine: twenty runs would take **13 to 42 weeks** on the Mac. The machine costs **about $1,499** (32 GB with 512 GB of storage, from reseller listings) and **about $7 a month in electricity** at its measured 55 W sustained draw (US average power price). Software: physics runs as plain MuJoCo on the main processor, brain B trains on the main processor rather than the graphics chip, small Dreamer goes to whichever of three published routes measures fastest, and large Dreamer and the camera need the graphics chip. Every speed for this workload is an extrapolation. Nobody has published MuJoCo, SAC or Dreamer timings on an M6. So the first day's twelve measurements (end of this report) matter more than any figure here.

## How to read the numbers in this report

Three kinds of number appear, and each is labelled. **Measured on an M6** means a reviewer timed the actual chip: benchmark scores, power, noise. **Measured on other hardware, then scaled** means a timing from an older Apple chip, an NVIDIA card or the project's own server, multiplied by a ratio. Almost every speed for Jack's workloads is of this kind. **Arithmetic** means hours and costs worked out from those speeds. "(Measured here)" marks the project's own timings on its 4-core ARM cloud server. They were taken while the box was busy with other work, so they slightly understate that server's speed.

| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| Core | One of the chip's independent workers. The M6 has 2 "super" and 4 "performance" cores, which are both fast, plus 6 slower "efficiency" cores |
| Decision | One action choice by Jack: 0.2 simulated seconds, made of 40 physics sub-steps |
| Integrator | The maths that advances the physics one sub-step. RK4 (today's) costs about 4 times a cheap one such as `implicitfast` |
| Gradient step / update | One adjustment of a brain's weights from one batch of replayed experience |
| Train ratio | Gradient steps per decision lived. Dreamer's published setting is up to one per decision |
| Graphics chip (GPU) | The part of the chip built for many parallel sums; it does the heavy maths of training |
| CUDA | NVIDIA's GPU software, which the T4 uses and which nearly all published code targets |
| MPS | PyTorch's route onto the Mac's graphics chip |
| MLX | Apple's own machine-learning library for its chips |
| JAX | Google's library in which the official Dreamer is written. On a Mac it runs only on the main processor |
| Launch overhead | A fixed cost paid every time the processor hands one small job to the graphics chip. It dominates when networks are small |
| Background priority (QoS) | A macOS label that confines a program to the slow efficiency cores |

The four research notes disagree in places. The table shows both figures and which is better sourced. In each case the report uses the better-sourced figure.

## The world and brain B run several times faster, on facts that are mostly second-hand

The chip itself is well measured. Notebookcheck ran an M6 Mac mini through Geekbench 6. It scored **4,698 on one core and 21,302 on all cores**, and the Cinebench all-core test held between 41.5 and 47.2 W ([Notebookcheck M6 analysis](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Apple-M6-SoC-Analysis-Apple-s-2-nm-chip-crushes-AMD-Intel-Qualcomm.1404057.0.html)). On the same test, a core of the Oracle Ampere server type the project uses scores about 1,100, and the 4-core server about 3,500 on all cores. A 4-core Kaggle session scored 786 and 1,687 ([Geekbench Browser, A1.Flex](https://browser.geekbench.com/v6/cpu/baseline/1787088); [VPSBenchmarks, Kaggle-type VM](https://www.vpsbenchmarks.com/yabs/google_compute_engine-4c-31gb-20260518-0b077c)). On paper, then, **one M6 core is about 4.3 Ampere cores, and the whole chip is about 6 of the project's servers or 13 Kaggle sessions**. That is arithmetic on measured scores.

Turning that into MuJoCo speed is where measurement stops. **No published MuJoCo timing exists for any M4, M5 or M6 processor core.** The only official Apple figure is about 650,000 humanoid steps a second on an M3 Max with every core busy ([MuJoCo MJX documentation](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/stable/mjx.html)). That works out to roughly 46,000–50,000 per fast core (arithmetic). The project measured about 20,000 per Ampere core. So on real MuJoCo the M3 is about 2.3–2.5 times an Ampere core, where Geekbench says 2.8: MuJoCo gets about 0.8–0.9 of the Geekbench advantage. Two causes make the gap plausible. Recent Geekbench versions use Apple's matrix hardware on some tests, and MuJoCo's step is plain, branchy code that cannot use it. Applying that discount to the M6's Geekbench lead gives **2.5–4 times an Ampere core per fast M6 core** (scaled; MuJoCo note). The figures below use it.

| Workload, one process on one fast core | Today on an Ampere core (measured here) | M6 estimate (scaled) |
|---|---|---|
| Raw physics, Jack's world, RK4 | 2,800 sub-steps/s | 7,000–12,500 |
| Raw physics, cheaper integrator | 10,600 sub-steps/s | 26,000–48,000 |
| Whole Python loop today, RK4 | 33–38 decisions/s | 85–150 |
| Whole loop today, cheaper integrator | 63 decisions/s | 160–250 |
| Whole loop after the planned fixes | not yet built | 300–800 |
| Six processes on the six fast cores, after fixes | — | 1,400–4,000 (+15–35% using the six slow cores too) |

The loop figures scale too, because the Python code around the physics is the same kind of one-core work that Apple cores do well. The "after fixes" row assumes the 40 sub-steps run as one native call, rays go out in one batch, and the Python bookkeeping per sub-step falls to 1–4 ms on an Ampere core. If that bookkeeping stays per sub-step in Python, the Mac stays near the "today" rows (MuJoCo note). The team's own estimate that the fixes bring a 4-core Kaggle session to 500–2,000 decisions a second is more optimistic than the MuJoCo note's per-process assumption. The robust number is the ratio. Whatever the fixes achieve, **the Mac should do about 4.5–8 times the 4-core server and 10–16 times a Kaggle session**, as long as each world runs as its own process (scaled; hardware note). The six fast cores do the heavy lifting. The efficiency cores add perhaps a fifth to a third, because when fast work spills onto them they run at roughly half a fast core's capacity ([Eclectic Light, Feb 2026](https://eclecticlight.co/2026/02/08/last-week-on-my-mac-why-e-cores-make-apple-silicon-fast/)).

Brain B's cost is its training updates, and here too the evidence is scaled. One update of the 2×256 network at batch 256 takes about 50 ms of an Ampere core in PyTorch (Stable-Baselines3) and about 20 ms in SBX, the JAX version (measured here). On an M6 fast core the notes estimate **3–15 ms in PyTorch on the processor (70–300 updates a second) and 2–7 ms in SBX (150–500 a second)**. The basis is a faster core plus Apple's matrix unit, which PyTorch reaches through Apple's maths library. Between the M4 and M5, PyTorch's processor speed on large matrix sums jumped 3.5–4 times in one public benchmark suite ([mlx-benchmark](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/TristanBilot/mlx-benchmark/main/benchmarks/average_benchmark.md)). Moving brain B to the graphics chip would not help. The project's maintainers say small networks belong on the processor ([SB3 PPO docs](https://stable-baselines3.readthedocs.io/en/master/modules/ppo.html); [SB3 issue 914](https://github.com/DLR-RM/stable-baselines3/issues/914)). A SAC update is a few hundred tiny jobs, and on an M3 Max each tiny job cost the graphics chip more than the processor ([PyTorch issue 138898](https://github.com/pytorch/pytorch/issues/138898)).

Put together for the decisive test (arithmetic): one run of a million decisions with SBX, one update per decision and today's loop on the cheaper integrator costs about 6–13 ms per decision, or **1.7–3.6 hours per run**. With two updates per decision it is 2.2–5.6 hours. The worst combination, PyTorch with two updates on the RK4 loop, is about 4–11 hours. The same run takes about 10 hours on an Ampere core. With six runs side by side on the fast cores, **all twenty runs finish in about 6–22 hours, or 4–40 hours across every combination**. The earlier plan budgeted 20–30 free Kaggle sessions, under a week on five of them. The Mac turns that into an overnight job.

## Dreamer is the bottleneck: slower than the T4 per hour, but on all week

Dreamer is expensive because of its learner, not its world. One gradient step replays 16 sequences of 64 steps, then imagines 15 steps ahead from 1,024 states. That is about 65 billion arithmetic operations for the 0.64M model and 980 billion for the 9.7M model (arithmetic). The published setting does up to one such step per decision. Doing fewer is known to cost score: one user report has Walker Walk falling from about 900 to 200–300 when the ratio dropped sixteenfold ([dreamerv3 issue 140](https://github.com/danijar/dreamerv3/issues/140)). Even on NVIDIA cards, the most-used PyTorch port runs at about half the GPU use of the JAX original ([sheeprl issue 336](https://github.com/Eclectic-Sheep/sheeprl/issues/336)). One A100 measurement put its small model at 32–348 ms per step. The T4 prediction of 20–50 steps a second for the 0.64M model is itself only arithmetic, and probably optimistic.

**No Dreamer code of any kind has been reported running on Apple silicon**: not the official one, not sheeprl, not dreamerv3-torch (training note). The Mac figures therefore lean on three pieces of evidence, none of them from an M6. First, the only clean training race between an Apple chip and a T4: on ResNet-50, the T4 ran **1.6–1.8 times faster than an M3 Pro** ([arXiv 2508.18206](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.18206v1)). By its synthetic scores the M6's graphics chip is roughly M3-Pro-class, about 70–80% faster than the M4's ([Notebookcheck review](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Compact-powerhouse-with-the-2-nm-M6-SoC-Apple-Mac-mini-2026-Review.1401148.0.html)). Second, the cost of small jobs. On an M3 Max a GRU cell, the recurrent unit at Dreamer's core, ran 2.1 times slower on the graphics chip than on the processor, and a hand-written minimal version 3.5 times slower ([PyTorch issue 138898](https://github.com/pytorch/pytorch/issues/138898)). Dreamer's ports build their recurrent unit by hand from small pieces, so that penalty applies. A Dreamer step is several thousand such small jobs, and at 15–30 microseconds each that adds roughly 75–300 ms of pure waiting per step (arithmetic). Third, MLX. On an M5 laptop it trained **1.3 times faster than PyTorch on the graphics chip at full precision and 2.5 times faster at half precision**, in a single author's test ([DDPM MLX vs MPS](https://github.com/ai-Priest/DDPM-Apple-MLX-vs-PyTorch-MPS)). It is also the only library Apple tunes for the M6's new per-core matrix units, which need macOS 26.2 or later ([Apple ML Research](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/exploring-llms-mlx-m5)).

| Route on the Mac | 0.64M: steps/s | Hours per 1M-decision run, full ratio / one step per 4 decisions | 9.7M: steps/s | Hours per run, full / one per 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Official JAX, processor only | 2–6 | 46–140 / 12–35 | 0.15–0.4 | 700–1,850 / 175–460 |
| PyTorch port (sheeprl), processor | 3–10 | 28–93 / 7–23 | 0.2–0.6 | 460–1,400 / 115–350 |
| PyTorch port, graphics chip (MPS) | 3–12 | 23–93 / 6–23 | 0.8–2.5 | 110–350 / 28–87 |
| MLX port (not yet written), graphics chip | 6–25 | 11–46 / 3–12 | 1.5–5 | 56–185 / 14–46 |
| *Free T4, for comparison (arithmetic)* | *20–50* | *6–14 / 1.4–3.5* | *2–5* | *56–139 / 14–35* |

All Mac rows are scaled estimates from the training note, deliberately wide. The hardware note is more optimistic, at 50–150 ms per small-model step (7–20 a second) and 0.3–0.4 s per large-model step on MPS. It computed from raw arithmetic capacity and did not count the small-job penalty, so it marks the hopeful edge, not the middle. Read across, **the Mac is about 0.2–0.6 times a T4 with PyTorch and 0.3–1 times with a compiled MLX port**. Each week of Mac time (168 hours) therefore equals roughly 34–100 T4-hours on PyTorch, 1.1–3.4 times the free weekly T4 quota (arithmetic).

For the decisive test the arithmetic runs as follows. Twenty runs of the 0.64M model at the full ratio cost **460–1,860 hours on the Mac's graphics chip, 3 to 11 weeks of continuous running**, against 112–280 T4-hours (4–9 weeks of quota). The two machines are complements, not rivals. The T4 finishes 2–5 runs a week and the Mac 2–7, so **together they complete the twenty in about 1.6–5 weeks**, where the T4 alone would take 4–9. A further gain may come from a second Dreamer run on the processor (JAX or PyTorch) while the first uses the graphics chip, since at these rates the world itself needs only a few percent of one core. That overlap is unmeasured, and the processor and graphics chip share one power budget of about 30 W on the processor side, so each would slow somewhat ([Notebookcheck review](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Compact-powerhouse-with-the-2-nm-M6-SoC-Apple-Mac-mini-2026-Review.1401148.0.html)). At one step per four decisions the Mac alone finishes the twenty in 0.7–3 weeks, but that ratio's score cost on Jack's task is unknown until the planned ratio experiment runs. **The 9.7M model at the full ratio is not affordable anywhere**: 2,200–7,000 Mac hours, or 1,100–2,800 T4-hours (37–93 weeks of quota). The Mac can run a handful of large-model runs, or twenty at a quarter ratio over roughly 3–10 weeks. The "few hundred machine-hours" in the plan holds for brain B on the Mac with room to spare. It holds for small Dreamer only at the optimistic end, or with MLX or a lower ratio.

Long continuous "lifetimes" are where the Mac changes the most. It has no 12-hour session cap and no weekly quota, so one life can simply run. A brain B creature at about 80–170 decisions a second lives **16–34 times faster than real time**: a 10-million-decision life, about 23 simulated days, takes roughly 16–35 hours. A Dreamer creature at the full ratio lives at 3–12 decisions a second, which is **0.6–2.4 times real time**. Each simulated day takes about half a day to a day and a half, so a 10-million-decision life takes 10–39 days on MPS (arithmetic). Memory is not the limit for vector senses. A million 74-number observations take about 0.3–0.6 GB, and ten runs fit in 32 GB (training note).

The camera "eye" adds two costs. Drawing a 64×64 frame should take about **0.5–3 ms per frame (300–2,000 frames a second per process)**, adding 10–50% to a fast decision. That figure is unverified: no rendering speed has been published for any Apple chip (MuJoCo note). Learning from pixels needs a convolutional encoder, and the official 12M-class Dreamer on 64×64 images has 10.5 million parameters ([r2dreamer issue 10](https://github.com/NM512/r2dreamer/issues/10)). So expect large-model speeds of about 1–3 steps a second on MPS. A million stored frames take about 12 GB, the real memory constraint on a 32 GB machine (hardware note). The eye also brings a hard practical rule. **MuJoCo's renderer on macOS hangs unless a user is logged in to the desktop.** A MuJoCo team member describes their own workaround as logging in by remote screen after every reboot ([dm_control issue 464](https://github.com/google-deepmind/dm_control/issues/464)). A ray-cast eye, for example a 16×16 grid of rays, needs neither the graphics chip nor a login and costs only microseconds per ray.

## Each workload has one software path, and each path has a known way to fail

The path for each workload follows from the evidence above and from the project's rule that published code comes before home-made code.

| Workload | Path on the Mac | Why | Most likely to break |
|---|---|---|---|
| Physics of the world | Native MuJoCo on the processor; one process per world, 6 on fast cores (up to 12 total); all 40 sub-steps in one call | GPU physics is not usable on a Mac: jax-metal is abandoned, MuJoCo's GPU version on a Mac falls back to the processor at about a sixth of native speed, and MuJoCo Warp is NVIDIA-only ([robomotic benchmark](https://github.com/robomotic/sim-benchmarks/blob/main/MACMJX.md); [MJX docs](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/stable/mjx.html)) | Workers silently demoted to slow cores by background priority or `nice` (2–3× loss, no error); results that are not bit-identical to the Linux server, so compare statistically |
| Brain B (SAC) | SBX on the processor, one thread per run, 4–6 runs side by side; SB3 on the processor as the correctness reference | Small networks are processor work; SBX is the published choice | JAX on the processor may not use Apple's matrix unit while PyTorch does, so SB3 could beat SBX on the Mac; measure both. Too many threads per run |
| Dreamer 0.64M | Measure three published routes and keep the fastest: official JAX on the processor, sheeprl on the processor, sheeprl on MPS | No route is proven on a Mac; JAX is fully fused and has the fewest Mac-specific risks | MPS slower than the processor (small-job penalty); **a silent wrong-gradient bug in recurrent layers on MPS filed four days ago against PyTorch 2.15** ([issue 204359](https://github.com/pytorch/pytorch/issues/204359)); `torch.compile` failing in Dreamer's sampling ([sheeprl issue 261](https://github.com/Eclectic-Sheep/sheeprl/issues/261)); 64-bit numbers unsupported on MPS |
| Dreamer 9.7M | sheeprl on MPS; an MLX port only if it first reproduces the published Dreamer scores | Too slow on the processor (0.2–0.6 steps/s); MLX is fastest on paper but no MLX Dreamer exists, a port is roughly 1,000–3,000 lines, and it counts as home-made | Memory creeping up over days (MPS caching, MLX's buffer pool), surfacing as slowdowns rather than crashes ([PyTorch issue 77799](https://github.com/pytorch/pytorch/issues/77799)) |
| Camera eye | MuJoCo's renderer in a logged-in desktop session, launched as a per-user LaunchAgent; or a ray-cast eye | Rendering needs a desktop session on macOS | The renderer hanging after a reboot with nobody logged in; whether MPS works under a boot-time service with no login is undocumented |

JAX's own install page says it is not supported on the Mac's graphics chip ([JAX installation](https://docs.jax.dev/en/latest/installation.html)). Its Apple plugin has had no release in over a year, and its developers say they are no longer working on it ([jax discussion 23362](https://github.com/jax-ml/jax/discussions/23362)). So the official Dreamer can run on the Mac only on the processor. Linux is no escape route either. Apple's virtual machines do not pass the graphics chip to Linux, and Asahi Linux does not support the M6 at all ([apple/container discussion 62](https://github.com/apple/container/discussions/62); [AppleInsider on Asahi](https://appleinsider.com/articles/26/08/29/asahi-linux-nears-m3-support-release-m4-and-m5-are-on-the-way)). Smaller porting traps from the operations note round this out. Python starts worker processes afresh on macOS rather than copying them, so launch code needs a main guard. The default disk ignores letter case. `/data` cannot be created at the root. Two libraries each bundling OpenMP can crash on import. cron needs Full Disk Access. Linux's `/proc`, `ionice` and core pinning have no direct equivalent, so worker counts must be capped in code.

## It costs about $1,500 to buy and $7 a month to run, and the same money buys a different machine

Apple sells two Mac minis today: the M6 and the M5 Pro. The M6 tops out at 32 GB, and **memory cannot be added later** ([Apple specs](https://www.apple.com/mac-mini/specs/)). The 32 GB / 512 GB model lists at **$1,499** at Abt, with AppleInsider's tracker showing $1,424–1,499 ([Abt listing](https://www.abt.com/Apple-Mac-mini-Compact-Desktop-M6-32GB-RAM-512GB-SSD-in-Silver-Z1UA0004B/p/248234.html); [AppleInsider prices](https://prices.appleinsider.com/mac-mini-m6)). One blog gives $1,299 for 32 GB / 256 GB, consistent with the $899 base plus a $400 memory upgrade ([BetterClaw](https://www.betterclaw.io/blog/mac-mini-m6-local-llm)). AppleInsider's review headline calls the M6 "badly hampered by too-small SSD" ([AppleInsider](https://appleinsider.com/articles/26/10/02/m6-mac-mini-review-excellent-hardware-badly-hampered-by-too-small-ssd)). Either way, checkpoints and replay files belong on an external drive, which saves writes on the soldered internal one. None of these prices was read on Apple's own store, so check there before ordering.

Running costs are measured, not guessed. Notebookcheck measured **2.1–2.4 W idle, a 74 W peak and a sustained plateau of about 55 W**, with the fan at 41.7 dB(A) at most ([Notebookcheck review](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Compact-powerhouse-with-the-2-nm-M6-SoC-Apple-Mac-mini-2026-Review.1401148.0.html)). Apple's own table gives 4 W idle and 70 W maximum ([Apple Support 103253](https://support.apple.com/103253)). At the 2026 US average of about 18 cents per kWh ([EIA via electricrates.org](https://electricrates.org/states/report/2026-06/)), 55 W around the clock is about 40 kWh and **$7 a month**. The 70 W worst case is about $9, and idling about $0.50 (arithmetic). For any other tariff, multiply watts by 0.73 to get kWh a month. No multi-day thermal test exists. The short loop tests show only a 12% spread in power, so heavy throttling is unlikely, but the 24-hour first-day test is the real check.

For the graphics-bound part, the notes priced three other ways to spend similar money. **An M5 Pro Mac mini with 48 GB costs $2,299, or $2,499 with the larger chip**, about $800–1,000 more. Its graphics chip is about 1.2–1.5 times the M6's and its all-core processor speed 1.35–1.6 times, but each core is about 9% slower. It still has no CUDA, JAX still runs only on the processor, and Apple rates its maximum draw at 145 W ([AppleInsider](https://appleinsider.com/articles/26/09/22/m5-pro-mac-mini-vs-m4-pro-mac-mini-compared-specs-hardware-price); [Apple Support 103253](https://support.apple.com/103253)). **A desktop with a used NVIDIA RTX 3060 12 GB** (about $290–355 used, on a $500–700 used platform) fits the same budget. In the ResNet test above, a laptop 3060 ran 8.1 iterations a second, against the T4's 6.4 and the M3 Pro's 4.0 ([arXiv 2508.18206](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.18206v1); [gpudojo](https://gpudojo.com/rtx-3060-12gb)). It would run the official JAX Dreamer on its graphics card unchanged, with the server's Linux scripts. **A new RTX 5060 Ti 16 GB** costs about $770–850 in November 2026, nearly twice its $429 launch price ([bestvaluegpu](https://bestvaluegpu.com/history/new-and-used-rtx-5060-ti-16gb-price-history-and-specs/)). Stated plainly, either desktop would likely be at least T4-class on Dreamer with no porting. It would also draw an estimated 200–300 W under load (about $26–39 a month), be louder and larger, and serve the other projects less well as a quiet, always-on box. Renting cloud GPUs was also priced in the notes. The owner has ruled it out, so it plays no part here. The Mac's case rests on the owner's stated needs: low power, silence, always on, shared use. On those it has no rival in this list. On raw Dreamer speed per dollar it does not lead.

## The first day: twelve measurements that replace every estimate

The owner asked to "get close", and the honest closeness is this: **on paper the chip is exact, but every workload number is one or two scaling steps from a measurement**. The first day should replace each of them. Two rules come before any timing. Workers must run at normal priority: never `nice 19`, never `taskpolicy -b`, and never a launchd job marked Background. The machine must not sleep or nap (`sudo pmset -a sleep 0 disksleep 0`, App Nap off, long runs under `caffeinate -dimsu`). Otherwise the numbers will measure macOS's power saving rather than the chip ([Eclectic Light on QoS](https://eclecticlight.co/2025/05/09/what-is-quality-of-service-and-how-does-it-matter/)). For the camera and possibly for MPS, a dedicated user should log in automatically. That requires FileVault off (or `fdesetup authrestart` before each planned reboot). An HDMI dummy plug helps screen sharing. Jobs that need the graphics chip run as LaunchAgents in that user's session, and processor-only services as LaunchDaemons ([Servey headless guide](https://servey.in/blog/headless-mac-mini-setup)).

| # | Measurement | Estimate it replaces |
|---|---|---|

## Conclusion

The purchase changes the plan's arithmetic less for Dreamer than for everything around it. Brain B, the simulator speed work and the stage 3 reproductions move from scarce free sessions to an overnight job on a machine that never times out. That frees the free T4 to do what only a CUDA card does well: Dreamer at full ratio. The Mac is best read as a second Dreamer lane of roughly equal weekly capacity, not a faster one. Running both lanes is what halves the A-arm's calendar, and that makes the planned train-ratio experiment the most valuable early result. If a quarter ratio costs little score on Jack's task, the Mac alone can finish the small-Dreamer decisive test in weeks. If it costs a lot, the T4 stays on the critical path.

The team will also be measuring things no one has published. No MuJoCo timing on an M4-or-later core, no SAC or Dreamer timing on any Apple chip, no rendering speed, no multi-day training soak and no Apple-against-T4 recurrent comparison exist in print. Day one's numbers will be the first anywhere, so they deserve the same care as any experiment: fixed versions, logged settings and a repeat a week later. They will replace every range in this report.
